Money never floats

Aman Kishore, Head of Product at TruKKer5 minute read

In this essay

  1. Derive balances, never store them
  2. The imbalance that FX rounding hid
  3. Pay at milestones, to the cent
  4. DSO is a ledger question
  5. The spec is eight tests long

Payment systems rarely lose money in one dramatic failure. They lose it a cent at a time. A carrier's cost is split three ways, each share is rounded on its own, and the carrier ends up owed a cent more than the agreed cost. A payout goes out against a cached balance that missed this morning's correction. Neither is worth a meeting. Over enough orders they add up to a month-end close that will not tie out, and a team whose whole job is finding out why.

Cent leaks in payment systems are how you end up with a reconciliation team.

I built settlement-engine to show the rules that keep that team small. It is a public repo on mock data: milestone-based carrier payouts, a multi-currency double-entry ledger, receivables aging and a DSO report, in standard-library Python with eight tests.

The first rule is the title. Money never floats. Most languages store a number like 2.675 as a float, a binary approximation, and the stored value sits a hair below 2.675. Ask Python to round it to two places and you get 2.67. That is a cent gone, in a direction nobody chose. In the engine every amount is a Decimal, rounded to the cent, half up, the moment it enters the ledger. The rounding is a rule somebody wrote down.

Derive balances, never store them

The ledger keeps entries and nothing else. Every entry debits one account and credits another for the same amount, so money can move but cannot be created or destroyed. When a carrier asks what they are owed, the engine adds up their entries. There is no balance column to update, so there is no balance column to drift.

A stored balance is a second copy of the truth. Two copies of the truth eventually disagree, and then somebody has to reconcile them. Deriving a balance costs a computation on every read. In return, the ledger cannot disagree with itself. That trade is cheap. At real volume you add a cache, but the cache is rebuilt from entries and nobody edits it by hand.

The imbalance that FX rounding hid

The trial balance is the simplest check in accounting: add up every account and the total must be zero. My first version of that check converted every balance to US dollars and then added them up. It hid a real imbalance behind FX rounding. I found the bug while building the repo, and the fix was to check each currency on its own.

The mechanism is dull, which is why it hides so well. Each balance is converted and rounded to the cent before the totals meet. At the demo rate a tenge is worth 0.0021 dollars, which rounds to zero cents. A book can be a tenge out of balance and still convert to a perfect zero. The report says balanced. The book is not.

Now the trial balance test posts entries in dirhams and tenge and asserts that each currency nets to exactly zero on its own. Reporting can convert to dollars. Control never should.

Pay at milestones, to the cent

In freight, carriers wait weeks past proof of delivery to get paid while customers stretch their own terms. Milestone settlements attack that gap. The engine accrues a carrier's earnings as the job happens: 30% at pickup, 50% at proof of delivery and 20% when the invoice is approved. Payouts can go out early against earnings already on the books, and the engine refuses any payout larger than the accrued balance.

That order splits cleanly. Plenty do not. Take a carrier cost of 9,999.99. 30% of it is 2,999.997, which rounds to 3,000.00. 50% is 4,999.995, which rounds to 5,000.00. 20% is 1,999.998, which rounds to 2,000.00. Add them up and the carrier has accrued 10,000.00 on a 9,999.99 job. Rounding each share on its own leaks a cent like that whenever the cost ends in 3, 5 or 9 cents: three costs in ten.

So the final milestone never computes a percentage. It takes the cost minus everything already accrued, posts 1,999.99, and the three accruals add up to the cost exactly. A pickup that fires twice is a data bug upstream, and the worst response is a quiet second accrual, so the engine refuses it, loudly. The production version needs idempotency keys on every event, because with enough volume duplicates stop being rare.

DSO is a ledger question

The demo runs a seeded four-month book: 18 orders, four carriers in four currencies, partial payouts, customer invoices and collections. It produces 65 ledger entries and a trial balance that holds in every currency. Open receivables come to 16,630.44 USD on 24,271.21 USD invoiced over 130 days, so days sales outstanding is 89.1 days. The aging report converts to dollars, which is fine. It is reporting.

DSO is the number a CFO watches, and it is a ledger question before it is a collections question. When invoices and collections are entries, aging is a query. When they sit in separate systems, each with its own balance, aging starts with a reconciliation.

This is the part of the repo closest to my day job. At TruKKer, the FinOps module I delivered, with real-time financial dashboards and workflows, took DSO from 145 days to 95. As Head of Product I transformed fintech and payables there with regulatory-compliant invoicing (ZATCA in Saudi Arabia, Uyumsoft in Turkey, 1C in Kazakhstan) and milestone-based wallet and bank payouts, eliminating payment leakages. Invoicing rules vary enough by country to be their own roadmap. Underneath sit milestone-based vendor settlements and multi-currency payments. None of the repo is company code. All of it reflects how I think.

The spec is eight tests long

The eight tests in the repo are the product spec for money, written so a machine can check it. The books net to zero in every currency. A negative amount or an unknown currency is refused. Milestones sum to the carrier's cost to the cent, 9,999.99 included. A milestone that fires twice raises an error. A schedule that does not add up to 100% cannot be created. A payout cannot exceed what has accrued. And 100 riyals convert to exactly 26.67 dollars, while a currency with no rate raises an error instead of guessing.

None of those sentences needs an engineer to translate it, and every one of them can fail a build. That is my bar for money logic: if I cannot walk a CFO through it on one screen, it is not done.

If you lead product, leave the code to engineering and keep the sentences. Who absorbs the odd cent is a policy decision. So is how far ahead of the work a carrier can be paid. Make those calls before the first payout, not after the first variance.
