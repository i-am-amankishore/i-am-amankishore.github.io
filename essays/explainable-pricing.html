Price the relationship, not the third decimal

Aman Kishore, Head of Product at TruKKer5 minute read

In this essay

  1. Four lines a human can argue with
  2. Where the freight knowledge lives
  3. The top of the curve is flat
  4. What fourteen lanes cannot tell you

A shipper asks why Riyadh to Jeddah costs more this month. The person on the call has nothing to offer but the number. At that point the quote is dead, however good the model behind it. Pricing engines that fail in the field usually fail here, not in the math.

Shippers are not the only skeptics. Ops teams and carriers do not trust black boxes either, and they are right not to. A quote you cannot explain is a quote you cannot defend or negotiate. You can only hold it or drop it. So freight-rate-simulator, a small pricing engine I wrote on mock data, opens with a contract: every quote is a sum of line items a human can argue with.

Four lines a human can argue with

Here is the engine's quote for a flatbed from Riyadh to Jeddah in June.

Every amount arrives with its reason, and each line moves independently. When the fuel price changes, the fuel line changes and nothing else does. If June turns out quieter than the seasonal curve assumed, you can point at the demand line and say how wrong it was.

That lets people argue one line at a time. A sales lead can defend the demand line to a shipper. A carrier can push back on fuel, and should whenever fuel moves. An argument about a line is a negotiation. An argument about a total is a standoff.

The reasons in the table are not written for this essay. The engine prints them from the same variables it adds up. An explanation written after the fact drifts away from the math. One printed by the math cannot.

There is a second payoff, in revenue. A lump sum can only be negotiated down. A list can also be added to. At Logisty, the B2C home and goods moving platform in the UAE whose 0 to 1 launch I led, value-added services and dynamic line-item pricing lifted average order value 9%, because the list gave the booking flow somewhere to put an upsell.

Where the freight knowledge lives

Base, fuel and demand are arithmetic anyone could write. The imbalance line is where knowing freight pays.

Trucks have to come home. On a lane where more loads go out than come back, the carrier who takes the outbound load will often drive the return leg empty, and someone pays for those empty kilometers. In my mock lane file, Riyadh to Jeddah is the heavy direction, with an imbalance of 0.30. The engine prices half of that empty-return risk into the fronthaul: 135.38 on a 902.50 base.

Run the reverse lane and the same physics becomes a discount. Jeddah to Riyadh carries an imbalance of minus 0.20, so the line comes out at minus 90.25, and the engine's reason reads "backhaul discount, trucks want this lane." A truck already in Jeddah would rather earn something on the way home than nothing.

This is the line a generic pricing model misses, because it treats every lane as its own market. Lanes come in pairs. Price them separately and you lose money going out, then lose the return load to whoever discounts it.

The top of the curve is flat

Every pricing review comes back to one question: price higher and win less, or price lower and win more. The simulator, simulate.py, answers it on a toy market. It builds a book of 500 shipments across the fourteen lanes and six truck types, draws a market rate for each load from our cost plus random noise, then sweeps our price from 1.00x to 1.30x of cost. At each level it estimates the chance of winning every load and adds up the expected profit.

At 1.00x we win 71.2% of loads at zero profit, a fine strategy for a charity. Profit climbs until 1.22x. There we win 32.7% of loads, about a third, and expected profit tops out at 27,751. Past that, each load we win earns more and we win too few of them. At 1.30x, the fattest margin in the sweep, profit is back down to 25,340.

Volume times margin is the business. Either one alone is a vanity metric.

The shape matters more than the peak. Every level from 1.18x to 1.26x lands within 5% of the peak, a band five price levels wide. Across it the win rate slides from 39.2% to 26.9%, while profit never falls more than 2.6% below the top. Step outside it in either direction and the loss passes 5%. A flat top is no accident: at the peak the slope is zero, so small moves either side cost little. What the simulator adds is how wide this top is.

Where the peak sits depends on numbers I chose: an elasticity of 0.10 and a market drawn as our cost plus noise. Change either and the peak moves. Anyone arguing the third decimal of that multiplier is arguing with my assumptions.

So inside the band, price the relationship. A shipper who gives you steady volume, or fills a backhaul you would otherwise run empty, gets the low end, and you win more of their loads. A one-off spot request gets the high end. My rule: show the band to whoever owns the account, let them choose inside it, and make anything below it a decision someone signs.

What fourteen lanes cannot tell you

The repo is not a machine learning model, on purpose. With fourteen lanes of mock data, learned pricing would be theater. A model fitted to invented lanes would only learn my assumptions back.

A real pricing desk needs what this repo fakes. Start with won and lost bid history by lane, truck and customer, the single most underrated dataset in freight. My simulator has random noise where that history should be. From it you measure elasticity per segment, instead of trusting one constant I picked. The constants at the top of rates.py, from fuel share to target margin, need a named owner and a change log, because they are pricing policy, not code. And the margin floor has to survive rule changes. Mine exists even though the current config can never trigger it.

When learned pricing does arrive, it has to beat this baseline line item by line item, so the quote stays a list someone can argue with. The model can find the peak. Choosing a point inside the band is a commercial decision, and it belongs to someone who knows the customer's name.
